Job scams target people at a vulnerable moment: when they need work, want flexible hours or are excited about an offer. The scammer's goal is usually one of three things: your money, your identity documents, or your bank account, which they want to use to move stolen funds.

Fake job offers can look polished. Scammers copy real adverts, borrow the names of well-known companies and use professional-sounding job titles. What gives them away is how the process works, not how the advert looks.

## The biggest red flag: you're asked to pay

A genuine employer pays you. It doesn't charge you to apply, train, get equipment or start work. Watch for:

- **Fees** for registration, training, certification, uniforms, starter kits or background checks.
- **Equipment cheques.** You're sent a cheque and told to deposit it, then pay a "preferred supplier" for a laptop or office equipment. The cheque later bounces and the money you sent is gone, and you may owe your bank the full amount. The FTC describes this fake-cheque pattern in its job scam advice ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams)).
- **Deposits to unlock earnings.** You're asked to top up an account, often in cryptocurrency, before you can withdraw what you've "earned".

## Other warning signs

- **You were offered a job you didn't apply for**, often by text, WhatsApp or Telegram. The FTC warned in 2026 about recruiter texts that simply ask you to reply "YES" ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/04/job-offer-text-probably-scam)).
- **There's no real interview**, or the "interview" happens entirely over a chat app with an unnamed HR manager.
- **The pay is far above normal** for little work and no experience.
- **You're asked for sensitive details early**: your Social Security, National Insurance, SIN or Tax File Number, bank login, or photos of your ID before you've had a genuine interview or a written offer.
- **The employer is vague.** No company name, no address, or a "client who wishes to remain confidential".
- **The recruiter uses a personal email address**, such as a free webmail account, rather than the company's domain.
- **You're pressured to accept immediately** because "slots are limited".

The UK's Disclosure and Barring Service and JobsAware have run awareness campaigns on similar signs, including vague adverts, being offered a job without meeting anyone from the company, and requests for money ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-year-jobseekers-urged-to-watch-out-for-7-signs-of-job-scams)).

## The main types of job scam

### Task scams

You're offered remote work rating products, liking videos or "optimising" orders in an app. Early tasks pay small amounts to build trust. Then you're told to deposit your own money, often crypto, to unlock more tasks or withdraw your balance. The FTC's advice is not to trust anyone who says they'll pay you to rate or like things online ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/11/task-scams-create-illusion-making-money)). Australia's Scamwatch describes the same pattern, including recruiters who move conversations to WhatsApp or Telegram ([Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/types-of-scams/jobs-and-employment-scams)).

### Money-mule jobs

The "job" involves receiving payments into your own bank account and forwarding them, keeping a commission. Titles such as "financial agent", "payment processor" or "remote cashier" are common. The money is usually stolen, and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre warns that the person moving it can be exposed to money-laundering charges ([CAFC](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm)).

### Fake cheque and reshipping jobs

Mystery shopper, caregiver and admin roles may involve depositing a cheque and sending part of it on. "Reshipping" or "package inspector" jobs ask you to receive parcels at home and post them elsewhere, often goods bought with stolen cards.

### Impersonated recruiters

Scammers pose as recruiters for real, well-known companies, sometimes on genuine job sites, to make an offer look credible. A real company name in the message doesn't mean the company sent it.

## How to verify an employer

1. **Find the company yourself.** Search for its official website instead of using links in the message, and check whether the role appears on its own careers page.
2. **Contact the company through official channels.** Call the main number on its website and ask whether the recruiter works there.
3. **Check the email domain.** Recruiters for established companies normally email from the company's own domain.
4. **Search for complaints.** Look up the company name with words like "scam" or "complaint".
5. **Insist on a real interview.** A phone or video call with a named person, ideally someone you can find on the company's website or verified profile.
6. **Get the offer in writing before sharing documents.** Provide ID and bank details only through a formal onboarding process after a written offer.
7. **Never pay to start.** Any request for money is your cue to stop.

## If you've already paid or shared details

- **Paid money?** Contact your bank or the payment provider immediately.
- **Shared bank details or ID?** Tell your bank, change any passwords you shared, and use the identity theft services for your country listed on our [country pages](/scam-prevention).
- **Received money into your account for someone else?** Stop moving money and contact your bank straight away.
- **Report it.** In the US, report to the FTC. In the UK, JobsAware can help with job scams, and fraud with losses goes to Report Fraud (or Police Scotland). In Canada, report to the CAFC. In Australia, report to Scamwatch.
