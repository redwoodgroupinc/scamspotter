Bank impersonation scams are effective because they turn your caution against you. The message or caller warns that your account is at risk, that someone has made a suspicious payment or that your money needs protecting, and then asks you to do something that hands control to the scammer.

These scams can be very convincing. Scammers can make a call appear to come from your bank's real number, and Australia's Scamwatch reports that fake texts can even appear in the same message thread as genuine ones from your bank ([Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/about-us/news-and-alerts/scam-alert-scammers-impersonating-banks-in-text-messages-phone-calls-and-emails)). So the only reliable approach is to check through a route the scammer can't control.

## What genuine banks say they won't ask you to do

Official advice from all four countries we cover is consistent:

- **Move money to a "safe" or "secure" account.** Banks and police never ask you to do this. The account belongs to the scammer. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre advises never transferring money to a so-called safe account at the direction of an unexpected caller ([CAFC](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/b-investigator-enqueteur-eng.htm)).
- **Share a one-time code.** The FTC says no caller, especially from your bank's fraud department, will ever ask for your verification code ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/06/got-call-about-fraud-activity-your-bank-account-it-could-be-scammer)).
- **Tell them your password or PIN.** Scamwatch reports that banks say they won't ask for online banking passwords or one-time codes over the phone.
- **Install remote-access software** so they can "fix" or "protect" your account.
- **Hand your card to a courier.** The CAFC describes scammers who say they need to collect your debit or credit card from your home as part of an "investigation".
- **Keep it secret from branch staff.** If a caller tells you not to explain to the bank why you're moving money, that's a scam.

## How to check a message or call safely

### 1. Don't use the contact details you were given

Don't tap links, call numbers or reply to the message itself. Don't call back the number that rang you, and don't trust caller ID: it can be faked.

### 2. Contact your bank through a route you choose

Use the phone number on the back of your card, your bank's official app, or its website typed in yourself. If there's a genuine problem, the bank will be able to see it.

### 3. Use a different phone, or wait

Some scammers keep a phone line open after you hang up, so your next call goes straight back to them. The CAFC suggests calling from a different phone if possible, or waiting 10 minutes before calling out.

### 4. In the UK, call 159

The 159 service from Stop Scams UK connects you to the fraud teams of most UK banks. Hang up, then dial 159 yourself. Stop Scams UK says 159 will never call you ([Stop Scams UK](https://stopscamsuk.org.uk/campaign/get-help-now/)).

### 5. Take your time

A genuine bank won't mind you hanging up to check. The UK's Take Five campaign puts it simply: stop and think, challenge the request (it's fine to refuse or ignore it), and protect yourself by contacting your bank directly ([Take Five](https://www.takefive-stopfraud.org.uk/)).

## What about genuine fraud alerts?

Banks do send real alerts. Some ask you to confirm a recent transaction by replying YES or NO, or ask you to call the number on your card. A genuine alert shouldn't ask you for your password, PIN or one-time code, or tell you to move money.

The safest rule works for both genuine and fake alerts: **if a message about your bank account asks you to do anything other than check your account, contact your bank yourself first.** The FTC has repeatedly warned about fake texts that claim to come from a bank's fraud department, so treat unexpected alerts with suspicion ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/04/unexpected-text-scam)).

## A typical script

Bank impersonation calls often follow this pattern:

1. A text or automated call warns of a suspicious payment.
2. A "fraud specialist" calls and already knows some of your details, which makes them seem genuine.
3. They say your account is compromised, perhaps by someone inside the bank, so you mustn't tell branch staff.
4. They ask you to read out a code "to verify you", install an app, or move your money to a "safe account".

Each step is designed to build trust and urgency at the same time. The moment money, codes or secrecy come up, hang up.

## If you've already acted

Call your bank immediately using the number on your card (or 159 in the UK) and explain what happened. Then follow our guide on [what to do after a suspicious message](/guides/what-to-do-after-a-suspicious-message), which covers shared codes, payments and remote access, and lists where to report it in your country.
