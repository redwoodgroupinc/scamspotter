Scam texts, sometimes called "smishing", work because text messages feel personal and immediate. They arrive on the same screen as messages from friends, they're short enough to read in a second, and a link on a small screen is hard to inspect. Scammers rely on you reacting before you think.

The good news is that most scam texts follow the same script. Once you know what it looks like, it becomes much easier to spot, even when the message is well written and uses a familiar company's name.

## The script behind most scam texts

Almost every scam text has three parts:

1. **A hook.** A problem (your parcel is held, your account is locked, you owe a toll) or a reward (a refund, a prize, a job).
2. **Pressure.** A deadline, a penalty or a threat that makes waiting feel risky.
3. **An action.** Tap a link, call a number, reply, or move the conversation to WhatsApp or Telegram.

The action is always something that takes you out of a channel you can check and into one the scammer controls. That's the moment to stop.

## Eight warning signs

### 1. You weren't expecting it

A delivery notice when you haven't ordered anything, a refund you didn't claim, or a prize for a competition you didn't enter. Scammers send messages in bulk, so they rely on a few people happening to be waiting for something.

### 2. A tiny fee or a surprisingly big reward

Small amounts, such as a £1.45 redelivery fee or a $4.15 toll balance, are designed to feel too minor to question. The real goal is your card number. Large rewards work the other way, by making you excited enough to skip the checks.

### 3. A deadline measured in hours

"Pay within 24 hours", "final notice" and "your account will be suspended today" are pressure tactics. Genuine organisations generally give you time and other ways to sort out a problem.

### 4. A link that isn't the organisation's real address

Look at the part of the address just before the first single slash. In `royalmail-redelivery.example/pay`, the domain is `royalmail-redelivery.example`, not Royal Mail. Watch for brand names with extra words, misspellings, unusual endings and **shortened links** such as bit.ly, which hide the destination completely. Our [Website Safety Guide](/website-safety-guide) explains how to read an address.

### 5. A request for a code, password or card details

One-time passcodes exist to prove that *you* are signing in or approving a payment. No bank, delivery company or government agency needs you to read one out or text it back. The same goes for passwords, PINs and full card numbers.

### 6. A "new number" from someone you know

"Hi Mum, I dropped my phone, this is my new number" is a well-known opener. The follow-up is usually an urgent bill or a request for money. Call your family member on the number you already have before doing anything.

### 7. A push to another app or a phone number

Scammers often try to move you to WhatsApp, Telegram or a phone call where they can apply more pressure. A message that asks you to call a "helpline" number it provides is just as risky as a link.

### 8. Details that don't add up

A text from a bank that comes from an ordinary mobile number or an email address, a generic "Dear customer", or a message about a service you don't use. Don't rely on spelling mistakes, though: many scam texts are perfectly written.

## What official organisations say

Knowing how genuine organisations behave makes fakes easier to spot:

- The **Canada Revenue Agency** says it doesn't send or request Interac e-Transfers, and that the only texts it sends are one-time sign-in codes. A CRA "refund" link by text is a scam ([CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/corporate/scams-fraud/recognize-scam.html)).
- **Services Australia** says it won't send you a link to sign in to myGov. Sign in by typing my.gov.au or using the official app ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/mygov-scams)).
- The UK's **National Cyber Security Centre** advises not clicking the link or installing any app if a "missed parcel" text prompts you to ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/scam-missed-parcel-sms-messages)).
- The US **Federal Trade Commission** recommends contacting the company through a phone number or website you know is real if you think a text might be genuine, rather than using the details in the message ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-report-spam-text-messages)).

## What to do when a scam text arrives

1. **Don't tap links, call numbers or reply.** Replying, even with STOP, can confirm that your number is active.
2. **Check independently if you're unsure.** Open the company's app or type its address yourself.
3. **Report it.** In the US, UK and Canada, forward scam texts to **7726** (SPAM), which passes them to your mobile provider. GOV.UK and Canada's Get Cyber Safe both describe the service as free. In Australia, report scam messages to [Scamwatch](/scam-prevention/australia).
4. **Block the sender and delete the message.**

Australia's Scamwatch sums up the approach in three words: Stop, Check, Protect. Pause before you act, check who you're really dealing with, and act quickly if something feels wrong.

## If you've already tapped the link

Tapping a link isn't always harmful on its own, but don't enter anything on the page that opened. If you entered a password, change it on the real site straight away. If you entered card details, call your bank. If your phone installed an app, the NCSC advises not logging in to any accounts on that device until it's been cleaned, and it explains when a factory reset is needed ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams)).
