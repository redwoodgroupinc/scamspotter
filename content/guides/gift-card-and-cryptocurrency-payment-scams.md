If someone asks you to pay a bill, a fine, a fee or a debt with gift cards or cryptocurrency, you can stop there: it's a scam. Few warning signs are as reliable. The US Federal Trade Commission says that anyone who demands payment by gift card is a scammer ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/avoiding-and-reporting-gift-card-scams)), and that only scammers demand payment in cryptocurrency ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-scams)).

## Why scammers want gift cards and crypto

Both payment methods share features that suit criminals:

- **They're fast.** Once you share a gift card's number and PIN, or send crypto, the value can be moved within minutes.
- **They're hard to trace back to a person.**
- **They're very hard to reverse.** The FTC notes that cryptocurrency payments typically lack the legal protections of credit and debit cards, and that you can usually only get crypto back if the person you paid sends it back.

Legitimate organisations have no need for any of this. They send invoices, take card payments and use official payment portals.

## What official agencies say

Tax and benefits agencies are among the most impersonated organisations, so it helps to know what they say they won't do:

- The **IRS** says it doesn't call to demand immediate payment using a specific method such as a prepaid debit card, gift card or wire transfer ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/tips-to-help-taxpayers-recognize-tax-scams)).
- **Social Security** says a government fee should never be paid with retail gift cards, cash, wire transfers, internet currency or prepaid debit cards ([SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/scam/)).
- The **Canada Revenue Agency** says it won't demand or pressure you into immediate payment by Interac e-Transfer, cryptocurrency, prepaid credit cards or gift cards ([CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/corporate/scams-fraud/recognize-scam.html)).

## The stories scammers use

The payment request usually comes at the end of a story designed to make it feel necessary:

- **Government impersonation:** you owe back taxes, your ID number is "suspended", or there's a warrant for your arrest that will be cancelled if you pay now.
- **Utility shut-off:** your power will be cut off today unless you pay immediately, sometimes by scanning a QR code at a crypto ATM.
- **Tech support:** your computer is infected or your account was hacked, and you need to pay for a fix or "refund" an overpayment.
- **Family emergency:** a relative has been in an accident or arrested and needs money now, and asks you not to tell anyone.
- **The boss's favour:** a manager emails asking you to buy gift cards for clients and send the codes.
- **Romance and investment:** a new online friend or partner introduces you to a "trading platform" or crypto opportunity with guaranteed returns.
- **Job and task scams:** you must deposit crypto to unlock commissions you've "earned". See [how to identify a fake job offer](/guides/how-to-identify-a-fake-job-offer).

## Specific warning signs

- You're told **which card to buy and where**, and to read out or photograph the numbers on the back.
- Someone **stays on the phone** while you go to the shop or the ATM.
- You're told to **lie to shop staff or bank staff** about why you're buying cards or withdrawing cash.
- You're sent a **QR code** to scan at a Bitcoin or crypto ATM.
- You're promised **guaranteed or unusually high returns**. The FTC says only scammers guarantee profits or big returns.
- An investment firm has contacted you out of the blue. In the UK, check the **FCA Warning List** and the Financial Services Register before investing. The FCA notes that a firm can still be a scam even if it isn't on the Warning List ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/scamsmart/about-fca-warning-list)).

Shop and bank staff are often trained to spot these situations. If a cashier asks why you're buying a large number of gift cards, take the question seriously.

## What to do if you've already paid

**If you paid with gift cards:**

1. Contact the company that issued the card as soon as possible, tell them it was used in a scam and ask whether the funds can be frozen or refunded. The FTC keeps a list of issuers' contact details on its gift card scams page.
2. Keep the cards and receipts.
3. Report the scam (see below).

**If you paid with cryptocurrency:**

1. Contact the exchange or service you used to send it and report the transaction.
2. Record the details: wallet addresses, amounts, dates, times and transaction IDs. In the US, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center asks for these when you report ([IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/)).
3. Report the scam, even if you don't have every detail.

**Then report it** in your country: ReportFraud.ftc.gov (and IC3) in the US, Report Fraud or Police Scotland in the UK, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre in Canada, and Scamwatch in Australia. Our [country pages](/scam-prevention) list each service.

**Watch out for recovery scams.** People who've lost money are often targeted again by "recovery services" promising to get it back for a fee. The FTC warns that recovery offers that ask you to pay by gift card, crypto or wire transfer are scams.
