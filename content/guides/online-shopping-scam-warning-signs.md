Online shopping scams range from entire fake shops to individual sellers on marketplaces who take payment and disappear. Some sell counterfeit goods; others send nothing at all. They often appear through social media adverts and sponsored search results, and they're especially common around big sales and holidays.

Most can be spotted with a few minutes of checking before you pay.

## Warning signs of a fake shop

### Prices that are too good to be true

Heavily discounted designer goods, electronics or "clearance" deals far below other retailers are the most common lure. Australia's Scamwatch lists unusually low prices compared with other websites as a key warning sign ([Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/types-of-scams/buying-and-selling-scams)).

### A new or odd web address

Check the domain carefully. Scam shops often use a brand name with extra words, an unusual ending, or a domain registered very recently. Our guide to [verifying a suspicious website](/guides/how-to-verify-a-suspicious-website) shows how to check a domain's age.

### Missing or copied business details

Look for a real business address, a phone number, clear delivery and returns terms and a privacy policy. Scamwatch warns that fake shops may lack terms and conditions or an ABN, and that some even use a .com.au address and a stolen ABN to look Australian.

### Reviews that don't add up

Only glowing reviews, reviews with no detail, or no independent reviews at all are warning signs. The FTC warns that star ratings can be faked and suggests searching the shop's name with words like "review", "complaint" or "scam" ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).

### Pressure to buy now

Countdown timers, "only 2 left" banners and messages from a seller urging you to pay before someone else does are designed to rush you.

### Unusual payment methods

This is the clearest warning sign of all. Requests to pay by bank transfer, cryptocurrency, gift cards or money transfer services, or into different accounts for different orders, mean you'll have very little chance of getting your money back.

## Marketplace and social media sellers

Buying from individuals on marketplaces and social media adds its own risks:

- **Stay inside the platform.** The FTC advises avoiding sellers who ask you to pay outside the marketplace's own payment system ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-online-marketplace)).
- **Be wary of sellers who won't let you see the item** or who make excuses about why they can't meet or show more photos.
- **Check the seller's profile**: how long it's existed, its history and its reviews.
- **Don't send a deposit** to "hold" an item for a seller you can't verify.

Scamwatch also describes social media stores that open briefly, take a number of orders, then vanish.

## Pay in a way that protects you

How you pay matters as much as where:

- **Credit cards** usually let you dispute a charge for goods that never arrive. The FTC highlights this right in the US, and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre notes that many credit cards protect online purchases under the Consumer Credit Act ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/shopping-online-securely)).
- **Established payment services** often have buyer-protection schemes. Check the terms before you rely on them. Scamwatch recommends paying by PayPal or credit card rather than direct bank deposit.
- **Avoid direct bank transfers to shops you don't know.** The NCSC advises never paying by direct bank transfer when shopping online.
- **Never pay with gift cards or crypto** for online purchases from a shop or seller you don't know.

## A two-minute checklist before you pay

1. Is the domain the shop's real address, and has it existed for more than a few months?
2. Can you find independent reviews or complaints?
3. Are there real contact details, returns terms and a privacy policy?
4. Is the price realistic compared with other retailers?
5. Can you pay by credit card or a payment service with buyer protection?
6. Did you find the shop yourself, rather than through an unexpected message or advert?

If you can't answer yes to most of these, shop somewhere else.

## Protect your accounts

The NCSC also recommends using a guest checkout where you can, not letting shops store your card details, using strong unique passwords and turning on two-step verification for important accounts.

## If something goes wrong

- **Contact the seller first** if it's a genuine shop with a delivery problem.
- **Contact your card issuer or payment service** to dispute the charge if goods never arrive or are fake. Do this promptly, because dispute windows are limited.
- **If you shared card details with a fake shop**, call your bank to block the card.
- **Report it**: to the FTC in the US, to Report Fraud (or Police Scotland) in the UK, to the CAFC in Canada and to Scamwatch in Australia. See [what to do after a suspicious message](/guides/what-to-do-after-a-suspicious-message) for details.
