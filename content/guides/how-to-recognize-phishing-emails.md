Phishing emails pretend to come from an organisation you trust, such as your bank, a delivery company, a tax agency, your email provider or even your employer, to get you to do something that benefits the scammer. That might be entering a password on a fake login page, paying a fake invoice, opening a malicious attachment or sending personal details.

Phishing has become harder to spot by appearance alone. Logos are easy to copy and many phishing emails are well written. The reliable clues are in what the email asks you to do and where it really comes from.

## The common hooks

Phishing emails usually tell a short story designed to make you act:

- **A problem with your account:** suspicious sign-in activity, a failed payment or an account about to be closed.
- **Money you're owed:** a refund, a rebate or compensation.
- **Something to open:** an invoice, a voicemail, a shared document or a contract to sign.
- **A request from someone you know:** a manager who needs a "quick favour" or a supplier with "new bank details".

The US Federal Trade Commission notes that phishing messages often claim there's suspicious activity, a problem with your account or payment, or a refund waiting for you ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams)).

## Warning signs to check

### The sender address doesn't match the organisation

Email apps often show only a display name such as "PayPal Service". Tap or hover over it to reveal the full address. Look for misspelled domains (paypa1, micros0ft), brand names on unrelated domains, or an organisation writing from a free personal mailbox. Sender addresses can also be forged, so a correct-looking address isn't proof either.

### Links lead somewhere else

On a computer, hover over a link without clicking to see its real destination. On a phone, press and hold. Focus on the domain just before the first single slash. If an email says it's from your bank but the link goes to an unfamiliar domain, don't use it. Our [Website Safety Guide](/website-safety-guide) explains how to read web addresses.

### It asks for credentials or payment details

Requests to "verify your account", "confirm your identity" or "update your payment details" through a link are classic phishing. Sign in through the organisation's app or by typing its address yourself instead.

### It pushes an unexpected attachment

Unexpected invoices, voicemails, delivery labels and shared documents are common lures. Be especially wary of archive files (.zip), web-page files (.html), and any document that tells you to "enable content" or "enable macros".

### It creates urgency

Deadlines such as "within 48 hours", warnings that your mailbox is full or your password is expiring, and threats of account closure are designed to make you act before you check.

### It says payment details have changed

An email from a supplier, landlord, builder or solicitor announcing new bank details is a common way of redirecting payments. Always confirm by phone using a number you already have, never one from the email.

### The greeting is generic

"Dear Customer" or "Dear Account Holder" suggests a mass mailing. Many genuine bulk emails do this too, so treat it as a weak sign on its own.

## A safe routine for any email you're unsure about

1. **Ask whether you were expecting it.** The FTC suggests asking whether you have an account with the company or know the sender. If not, it's likely phishing.
2. **Don't use links, attachments or contact details in the email.**
3. **Go to the source.** Open the organisation's app, use a bookmark, or find its phone number on a statement or official website.
4. **If it's from a colleague or supplier**, confirm through a different channel, such as a phone call to a number you already know.

## Government impersonation

Tax and benefits agencies are frequent targets. The IRS asks people to send fake IRS and Treasury emails to phishing@irs.gov and not to click links or open attachments ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/help/report-fraud/report-fake-irs-treasury-or-tax-related-emails-and-messages)). The Australian Taxation Office asks for suspicious emails to be forwarded to ReportScams@ato.gov.au and offers a phone line to check whether contact is genuine ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/online-services/scams-cyber-safety-and-identity-protection/verify-or-report-an-ato-scam)).

## Where to report phishing emails

- **United Kingdom:** forward to **report@phishing.gov.uk**, the NCSC's Suspicious Email Reporting Service ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/report-suspicious-emails-websites-phishing)).
- **United States:** the FTC recommends forwarding phishing emails to **reportphishing@apwg.org** and reporting them at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
- **Canada:** report phishing to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre ([CAFC](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/phishing-hameconnage-eng.htm)).
- **Australia:** report to Scamwatch. Fake ATO emails can go to ReportScams@ato.gov.au.

You can also use your email provider's "report phishing" or "report junk" option, then delete the email.

## If you clicked a link or opened an attachment

If you entered a password, change it on the real site and anywhere else you've used it, and turn on two-step verification. If you entered card or bank details, call your bank. If you opened an attachment or installed something, run a security scan and follow the NCSC's advice on what to do next ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams)). Our guide on [what to do after a suspicious message](/guides/what-to-do-after-a-suspicious-message) covers each situation.
