Fake websites are built to look exactly like the real thing: the same logo, the same colours, sometimes even a copied layout. You usually reach them through a link in a text, an email, a social media advert or a sponsored search result. Because the design can be copied perfectly, the most reliable checks focus on things a scammer can't easily fake: the domain name, its history and how the site wants you to pay.

## Step 1: Find the real domain

Every web address has a **registrable domain**: the name just before the first single slash, plus its ending (.com, .co.uk, .ca, .com.au and so on). That's the part an organisation registers and controls.

- In `https://login.example.co.uk/account`, the domain is **example.co.uk**.
- In `https://paypal.com.secure-check.example/signin`, the domain is **secure-check.example**. The "paypal.com" at the start is just a label the scammer chose.
- In `https://example.com/amazon/login`, the domain is **example.com**. Anything after the slash can say anything.

If the domain isn't the organisation's real one, stop there.

## Step 2: Look for lookalike tricks

Scammers register domains designed to pass a quick glance:

- **Misspellings and swapped characters:** "paypa1" with a number one, "arnazon" where "rn" imitates "m", or an extra or missing letter.
- **Extra words:** the brand name plus "secure", "login", "support", "refund" or "delivery".
- **A different ending:** the right name with an unusual domain ending.
- **International characters:** letters from other alphabets that look identical to Latin ones. Browsers may show these in an encoded form starting with "xn--".
- **Hidden destinations:** shortened links, or an "@" sign in an address, which makes browsers ignore everything before it.

Our [Website Safety Guide](/website-safety-guide) checks an address for these patterns without visiting it.

## Step 3: Don't rely on the padlock

The padlock and "https" mean your connection is encrypted. They say nothing about who runs the site. Certificates are free and quick to obtain, and scam sites use them routinely. Citizens Advice puts it plainly: a padlock doesn't guarantee you're buying from a real company ([Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/scams/check-if-something-might-be-a-scam/)).

Treat a missing padlock as a reason not to enter details. Never treat its presence as a reason to trust the site.

## Step 4: Check how old the domain is

A bank, a well-known retailer or a government service will usually have had its domain for many years. A shop that appeared last month deserves extra caution. [ICANN Lookup](https://lookup.icann.org/) shows registration data for many domains, often including the date it was created. Some registries limit what they publish, so a missing date isn't proof of anything on its own.

## Step 5: Check whether it's been flagged

Google's [Safe Browsing site status](https://transparencyreport.google.com/safe-browsing/search) tool shows whether Google currently considers a site unsafe. A clean result only means it hasn't been flagged yet. New scam sites are often taken down and replaced quickly, so they may not appear in any list.

## Step 6: Look for independent evidence

- **Search for reviews outside the site.** Search the shop's name with "reviews", "complaints" or "scam". The FTC suggests the same and warns that star ratings can be faked ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).
- **Check contact details and policies.** Look for a physical address, a working phone number, clear delivery and returns terms and a privacy policy. Australia's Scamwatch lists missing terms and conditions, ABN or privacy policy among the warning signs of fake shops ([Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/types-of-scams/buying-and-selling-scams)).
- **Be careful with search adverts.** Scamwatch has warned that scammers pay to place fake websites at the top of search results, so the first listing isn't necessarily genuine.

## Step 7: Watch how it wants you to pay

Requests to pay by bank transfer, cryptocurrency, gift cards or money transfer services are major warning signs on a shopping site, because the money is very hard to recover. Credit cards and well-known payment services usually offer a way to dispute a charge. See our guide to [online shopping warning signs](/guides/online-shopping-scam-warning-signs).

## Step 8: Use your own route in

The simplest protection of all is to avoid the question. If a message tells you to log in to your bank, your tax account or a delivery company's site, close it and use the official app or a bookmark instead.

## If you've found a fake website

- **UK:** report it to the National Cyber Security Centre's [suspicious website reporting service](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/section/about-this-website/report-scam-website).
- **US:** report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
- **Canada:** report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
- **Australia:** Scamwatch's report form includes an option for reporting a website.

If you've already entered a password or payment details, see [what to do after a suspicious message](/guides/what-to-do-after-a-suspicious-message).
