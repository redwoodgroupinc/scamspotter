If you've received a message you're not sure about, the right next step depends on what has happened so far. Find the situation that matches yours below. If more than one applies, start with the most serious: anything involving money or bank details comes first.

Acting quickly matters. Banks and payment providers have the best chance of stopping or recovering a payment when they hear about it straight away. And you have nothing to be embarrassed about: scams are designed by professionals to fool people, and reporting helps protect others.

## You haven't responded yet

This is the best position to be in.

1. **Don't click, call or reply.** Leave links, attachments and phone numbers in the message alone.
2. **Check independently if it might be real.** Contact the organisation through its app, a bookmark, or a phone number from its official website or your card.
3. **Report it.** See the reporting options for your country at the end of this guide.
4. **Block and delete.**

If you'd like a second opinion first, paste the message into our [Scam Message Checker](/message-checker) or [Email Scam Checker](/email-checker).

## You clicked a link but didn't enter anything

Close the page. Visiting a page doesn't usually cause harm on its own, but stay alert:

- If a download started or you were asked to install an app or profile, don't open or install it.
- Make sure your phone or computer and browser are up to date.
- If you're worried something was installed, run a security scan and follow the National Cyber Security Centre's advice on recovering a device ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams)).

## You entered a password

1. **Change the password on the real site straight away**, by typing the address yourself or using the official app.
2. **Change it anywhere else you've used the same password.**
3. **Turn on two-step verification** for that account.
4. **Check the account** for changes you didn't make, such as a new forwarding rule in your email, a changed phone number or unfamiliar devices.

## You shared card details, bank details or a one-time code

**Call your bank or card provider now**, using the number on the back of your card or in its official app, not a number from the message. Tell them exactly what you shared. They can block cards, watch for suspicious payments and advise you.

In the UK, you can dial **159** to be connected to most banks' fraud teams ([Stop Scams UK](https://stopscamsuk.org.uk/campaign/get-help-now/)). The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre suggests calling from a different phone, or waiting a few minutes, in case a scammer has kept the line open ([CAFC](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/b-investigator-enqueteur-eng.htm)).

## You sent money

Speed matters most here.

- **Bank transfer or card payment:** contact your bank immediately and ask whether the payment can be stopped, recalled or disputed.
- **Gift cards:** contact the company that issued the card straight away and ask whether the funds can be frozen. Keep the cards and receipts. The FTC keeps a list of gift card issuers' contact details ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/avoiding-and-reporting-gift-card-scams)).
- **Cryptocurrency:** contact the exchange or service you used and report the transaction. In the US, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center asks for details such as wallet addresses, amounts, dates and transaction IDs ([IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/)).
- **Wire or money transfer service:** contact the company you used immediately.

Then report the crime (see below).

## You gave someone remote access or installed an app

1. **Disconnect the device from the internet** (turn off Wi-Fi and mobile data).
2. **Don't log in to banking or email on that device** until it's been checked.
3. **Use another device to change important passwords** and contact your bank.
4. **Get trusted help** to remove the software. The NCSC explains when a full factory reset is the safest option.

## You shared ID details, such as a passport or ID number

Criminals can use identity details to open accounts in your name.

- **US:** use [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) to get a personal recovery plan.
- **Australia:** contact [IDCARE](https://www.idcare.org/), the national identity and cyber support service, on 1800 595 160.
- **UK and Canada:** tell your bank, report the fraud (below) and watch your credit reports and accounts for anything unusual.

## Beware of "recovery" scams

After a scam, some people are contacted by someone offering to get their money back for a fee, sometimes pretending to be police, a lawyer or a government agency. The CAFC advises never paying anyone upfront to recover money you lost to fraud ([CAFC](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/b-investigator-enqueteur-eng.htm)). Genuine agencies don't charge you to investigate.

## Where to report

| Country | Where to report |
| --- | --- |
| United States | [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). Internet crime with losses: [IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/). Scam texts: forward to 7726. |
| United Kingdom | England, Wales and Northern Ireland: [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) or 0300 123 2040. Scotland: Police Scotland on 101. Scam emails: report@phishing.gov.uk. Texts: 7726. |
| Canada | [Report Cybercrime and Fraud](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) or the CAFC on 1-888-495-8501, plus your local police. |
| Australia | [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/report-a-scam). Cybercrime: ReportCyber at cyber.gov.au/report. |

Our [country pages](/scam-prevention) list more reporting routes, including ones for tax-agency impersonation.

## Look after yourself

Being targeted by a scam can be stressful, whether or not you lost money. Talk to someone you trust, and remember that scammers are skilled at what they do. Reporting it, even if you didn't lose anything, helps authorities warn others.
